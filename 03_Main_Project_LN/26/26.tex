\section{Introduction}
% ============================================================

The automatic classification of clinical documents by medical specialty is a practical challenge with direct implications for healthcare administration, electronic health record management, and clinical decision support. Clinical notes are written in highly specialised medical language, vary enormously in length and structure, and are produced under time pressure, resulting in noisy, heterogeneous text that is difficult to process with general-purpose tools.

This work addresses the task of automatically predicting the medical specialty of a clinical record given its textual content. The dataset comprises 2{,}613 annotated records distributed across 12 specialties, where each record contains multiple associated text fields: a short description, a procedure name, a full clinical transcription, and a list of keywords. The dataset is characterised by a severe class imbalance, with the majority class (Surgery) comprising 33.5\% of all samples and the minority class (Dermatology) comprising only 1.0\%.

\textbf{Research Question:} Does fine-tuning a domain-specific language model (\ac{clinicalbert}) on short clinical descriptions yield higher classification accuracy than classical algorithms trained on the full, concatenated clinical records? This question is motivated by a fundamental trade-off in \ac{nlp}: complex models may extract deeper linguistic patterns from limited text, whereas simpler models may compensate through the sheer volume of information available in longer fields. Answering this question guides our choice of models, our experimental design, and the interpretation of our results.

\subsection{Data Analysis}

The training set consists of 2{,}613 clinical records sourced from a public medical transcription dataset~\cite{mtsamples}. Each record contains five fields: \texttt{medical\_specialty} (the target label), \texttt{description} (a short one-to-two sentence summary of the visit), \texttt{sample\_name} (the procedure or visit type), \texttt{transcription} (the full clinical note), and \texttt{keywords} (a comma-separated list of relevant terms).

\textbf{Class distribution.} Table~\ref{tab:class_dist} shows the distribution of samples across the 12 specialties. The dataset exhibits severe class imbalance: the majority class (Surgery) contains 876 samples, whereas the minority class (Dermatology) contains only 25 samples --- a ratio of approximately 35:1. Such imbalance is known to cause classifiers to default to frequent classes, resulting in near-zero recall for minority classes \cite{imbalance_survey}.

\textbf{Observation 1 --- Field length varies substantially across specialties.} The \texttt{description} field is short and consistent across all classes (overall mean: 28.4 words), making it suitable as input for models with strict token limits. The \texttt{transcription} field is significantly longer (overall mean: 452.2 words) and exhibits high inter-class variance: Radiology notes average 275.8 words, whereas Psychiatry-Psychology notes average 823.7 words. This disparity is directly relevant to the research question: a classical model trained on the full transcription will process up to three times more tokens for certain classes than for others, potentially introducing a length bias that favours verbose specialties.

\textbf{Observation 2 --- Vocabulary overlap between related specialties introduces classification ambiguity.} Analysis of the most frequent terms in the \texttt{description} field reveals significant lexical overlap between Surgery and Neurosurgery (shared top terms: \emph{right}, \emph{left}, \emph{anterior}, \emph{bilateral}, \emph{patient}), suggesting that word-frequency models may struggle to separate these two classes without attending to specialty-specific terms such as \emph{discectomy}, \emph{cervical}, or \emph{fusion}, which are present in Neurosurgery but rare in general Surgery. In contrast, Radiology contains highly distinctive vocabulary (\emph{MRI}, \emph{contrast}, \emph{ultrasound}, \emph{spine}, \emph{brain}) with limited overlap with other specialties, which may explain why it is easier to identify despite having only 217 training samples. Additionally, 30 records contain empty transcription fields and 158 records contain descriptions of fewer than five words, representing potential sources of noise in models relying on these fields.
